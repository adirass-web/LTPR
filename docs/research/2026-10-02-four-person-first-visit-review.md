# Research basis: four-person first-visit website review

This note is a method reference, not an evaluation of the site. It applies to four separate, moderated sessions with people encountering the site for the first time.

## What this sample can and cannot tell us

- Four participants fit GOV.UK's usual **4–8 people per qualitative usability-testing round**. Use this round to find and explain problems, then iterate and test again. It is not a representative survey, conversion benchmark, or basis for population percentages. [GOV.UK: planning research](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/plan-user-research-for-your-service); [NN/g: qualitative versus quantitative sample sizes](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-many-test-users/).
- Define the target audience before recruiting. Select actual or likely users with relevant differences in goals, familiarity, device habits, digital confidence, and access needs. Four people cannot cover every distinct audience; record gaps and arrange another round for materially different groups. [GOV.UK: finding participants](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/find-user-research-participants); [NN/g: when more user groups need testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-many-test-users/).
- Run participants independently and use the same core guide, tasks, and starting conditions, without letting later participants hear earlier feedback. The independence recommendation is a practical inference from the aim to capture each person's first encounter; a practice run with a colleague helps check task wording and setup. [GOV.UK: moderated usability testing](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-moderated-usability-testing); [GOV.UK: plan a round](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/plan-round-of-user-research).

## Session pattern

1. **Set expectations and consent.** Explain that the site, not the person, is being tested; participation is voluntary. Say who will observe, how notes or recordings will be used, and obtain informed consent before recording or note-taking. Arrange any accommodations beforehand. [GOV.UK: moderated testing](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-moderated-usability-testing); [GOV.UK: consent](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/getting-users-consent-for-research); [GOV.UK: notes and recordings](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/taking-notes-and-recording-user-research-sessions).
2. **Capture an unprimed first impression before tasks.** Open the same initial page and ask, for example, “What do you think this site is for?”, “What stands out?”, and “What would you try next?” Record the person's own words and first action. A short timed exposure is an option if the question is specifically about immediate visual impression, but it is distinct from testing whether the interactive site helps someone complete a goal. [NN/g: first-impression and visual-design methods](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/testing-visual-design/); [NN/g: first impressions during usability tests](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-impressions-human-automaticity/).
3. **Observe realistic tasks.** Give a believable goal, not a sequence of clicks or the label of the intended control. Ask the person to think aloud; then mostly watch and listen. Record where they go, what they expect, hesitations, errors, recovery, and whether they reach the goal. Pilot the tasks and keep a consistent discussion guide. [GOV.UK: moderated usability testing](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-moderated-usability-testing).
4. **Probe without supplying an answer.** Useful prompts include “What were you expecting there?”, “What makes you say that?”, and “What was easy or difficult about that?” Avoid “Was the navigation confusing?” or naming a suspected fault: leading wording can change feedback and subsequent behavior. Ask for clarification after observing, rather than coaching during the task; intervene only when needed to keep the session moving. [NN/g: leading questions](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/leading-questions/); [GOV.UK: moderated usability testing](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-moderated-usability-testing).
5. **Optional quick rating.** Immediately after each attempt, ask one consistently worded perceived-ease question, such as “Overall, how difficult or easy was this task to complete?” on a seven-point difficult-to-easy scale. Keep the individual ratings alongside behavior and explanations; with four people, do not treat a mean, percentage, or change in score as a reliable population estimate. A post-task rating measures perceived ease, not observed success or accessibility. [NN/g: Single Ease Question](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/measuring-perceived-usability/); [NN/g: sample-size limits](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-many-test-users/).

## Inclusion and interpretation

- Include people with disabilities and access needs in research for a broad audience; let them use familiar assistive technology or arrangements where practical. One person's experience cannot stand in for everyone with the same disability. User sessions can reveal barriers, but **do not establish WCAG conformance**; pair them with standards-based accessibility evaluation. [GOV.UK: planning inclusive research](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/plan-user-research-for-your-service); [W3C WAI: involving users in accessibility evaluation](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/involving-users/).
- Keep raw evidence separate from interpretation: write what participants did or said, with participant and task IDs, then group observations into themes, state the finding, and decide what to change or investigate next. Preserve surprising or isolated barriers even when they are not a repeated theme, especially high-impact failures. [GOV.UK: taking notes](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/taking-notes-and-recording-user-research-sessions); [GOV.UK: analysing sessions](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/analyse-a-research-session).
- In the readout, state recruitment criteria, relevant participant characteristics, device and assistive-technology context, tasks, method, observed evidence, and limitations. Phrase counts as “2 of 4 participants in these sessions,” not “50% of users.” Avoid treating an absence of observed problems as proof of usability or accessibility. [W3C WAI: drawing conclusions from limited evaluations](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/involving-users/); [NN/g: qualitative versus quantitative sample sizes](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-many-test-users/).
